Method questions

1. Why must the Benedict’s test be heated — and why a water bath rather than a flame?

The reaction between reducing sugar and Benedict’s reagent only happens at high temperature. A water bath keeps the temperature constant (80 °C) and safe — an open flame could overheat or crack the tube.

2. In the Biuret test, why is NaOH added before the copper sulfate?

NaOH makes the solution alkaline — the Cu²⁺ ions only form the purple complex with peptide bonds in alkaline conditions.

3. Why do we shake the food with ethanol first in the emulsion test, instead of adding water directly?

Fat dissolves in ethanol but not in water. When the ethanol solution is poured into water, any dissolved fat comes out of solution as tiny droplets — the cloudy white emulsion. No fat = stays clear.

4. A student tests biscuit crumbs and gets a brick-red Benedict’s result AND a blue-black iodine result. What does the biscuit contain?

Both reducing sugar and starch. Foods are mixtures — a food can give positive results for several tests. (Biscuit = flour starch + added sugar.)
