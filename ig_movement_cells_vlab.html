EXPERIMENTAL SKILLS 3.1

Watching diffusion happen

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient — a result of their random movement. Press remove the barrier and watch the particles. Press it a few times to repeat.

Observe and explain

1. Describe what happens to the blue particles after the barrier is removed.

They spread from the side where there are many (high concentration) into the side where there are few (low concentration), until they are roughly evenly spread. No energy is needed — particles move randomly.

2. Do particles stop moving once they are evenly spread?

No — they keep moving randomly in all directions. But there is no net movement any more: equal numbers cross in each direction. This is called equilibrium.

3. Give one example of diffusion in a living organism.

Oxygen diffusing from the air in the alveoli into the blood (high → low concentration), or carbon dioxide diffusing out of respiring cells into the blood.
EXPERIMENTAL SKILLS 3.2

Investigating osmosis using potato cylinders

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules across a partially permeable membrane, from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential. Potato cells will gain or lose water depending on the sucrose solution around them — and you can detect this by weighing the cylinders.

Method:

  • Pour: drag each bottle of sucrose solution onto an empty beaker to fill it (watch it pour!)
  • Weigh: drag a potato cylinder onto the balance and press “record mass”
  • Soak: drop the weighed cylinder into a beaker, then press “wait 30 minutes”
  • Re-weigh: take each cylinder out, weigh again, and record
  • Plot: press “plot my results” to draw the graph and find where the line crosses zero
Real-lab safety: take care cutting cylinders with a scalpel — cut on a tile, away from fingers. Dry each cylinder gently with paper towel before weighing (why? see Q3).

Analysis questions

1. In which solution did the cylinders gain the most mass? Explain why, using the term water potential.

Distilled water (0.0). Pure water has the highest water potential, so water moved by osmosis into the potato cells (lower water potential inside), across the partially permeable cell surface membrane. The cells became turgid.

2. What does the concentration where the line crosses 0% change tell you?

That solution has the same water potential as the potato cells — there is no net movement of water, so mass stays the same. It estimates the water potential (≈ concentration of the cell sap) of the potato tissue.

3. Why must you dry the cylinders before weighing, and why is % change in mass better than just change in mass?

Drops of solution on the surface would add mass that is not water inside the cells (a source of error). % change is fair because the cylinders did not all start at exactly the same mass — a 0.2 g gain means more for a 1.8 g cylinder than a 2.4 g one.

4. A cylinder is left in strong salt solution overnight. Describe the state of its cells.

Water leaves the cells by osmosis; the vacuole shrinks and the cytoplasm pulls away from the cell wall — the cells are plasmolysed and the tissue feels floppy (flaccid).
