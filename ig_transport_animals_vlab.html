EXPERIMENTAL SKILLS 9.1

Investigating the effect of physical activity on heart rate

In this investigation you will measure a pulse rate before and after exercise. You will practise measuring carefully, recording results clearly and using them to draw a line graph.

You will need:

  • this virtual student (no running required — the simulation supplies the exercise)
  • the timer built into the page
Safety: In the real investigation: if exercise might make you ill, do not do it — measure someone else’s pulse instead. In this virtual version, nobody gets tired.

Method

  1. Construct a results table (the page builds it for you).
  2. Let the virtual student sit still and relax. Measure the resting pulse three times.
  3. Start one minute of vigorous exercise.
  4. When exercise stops, measure the pulse again every 2 minutes until it returns to the resting value.
  5. Draw a line graph: time (min) on x, pulse rate (bpm) on y. Mark where exercise began and ended.

Reminder: what is a pulse?

A pulse is the expansion and relaxation of an artery, caused by the heart pushing blood through it. Pulse rate = heart rate. You can feel it in the wrist or beside the big tendons in the neck.

Teacher tip: run it once as a class demo, then let students predict the curve for a fitter student before revealing it.

Questions (coursebook, Experimental skills 9.1)

1 · Describe, in words, how exercise affected the pulse rate.

Use your table or graph: the pulse rate rises steeply during exercise, reaches a peak just after exercise stops, then falls gradually back towards the resting value. Quote two numbers from your own results (e.g. “from 72 bpm at rest to 150 bpm”).

2 · Explain why the pulse rate increases when you exercise.

Chain the ideas: muscles contract harder and faster → they respire more (aerobic respiration) → they need more oxygen and glucose, delivered by the blood, and produce more carbon dioxide to remove → the heart beats faster to deliver and remove these substances more quickly.

3 · Why does the pulse rate not fall back to normal immediately after exercise stops?

The muscles’ extra carbon dioxide must still be cleared and the body must “repay” the oxygen shortfall built up during exercise — so breathing and heart rate stay high for a few minutes. (You will meet the details in Chapter 11, Respiration.)
Teacher notes — how the simulation works

Each virtual student has a resting pulse (with small natural variation), a peak exercise pulse and a recovery speed. Fitness lowers the resting rate, lowers the peak and shortens recovery — matching the pattern students will meet in their own data. Readings include ±2 bpm measurement noise so the class must still think about repeats and means. No data leaves the page; everything runs locally in this file.
