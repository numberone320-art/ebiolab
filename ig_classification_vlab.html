EXPERIMENTAL SKILLS 1.2

Observing features used in classification

Classification keys use observable features — things you can see: number of legs, wings, body covering. Click on each labelled feature of the specimen to observe it, then answer the questions below.

Specimen: a beetle (insect). Click each glowing part to observe it.

Observation questions

1. How many legs does the beetle have?

Six legs. All insects have exactly 6 legs — this is the fastest feature for recognising the group. Spiders (arachnids) have 8.

2. How many body sections can you see?

Three: head, thorax and abdomen. The legs and wings are attached to the thorax.

3. Why do biologists prefer visible features (not, for example, “it eats leaves”) for keys?

A key must work on the specimen in front of you — even a dead one. Behaviour, diet and habitat often cannot be observed directly, but legs, wings and body covering always can.

Key questions

1. Write the key route you took for specimen A (e.g. 1b → 3a)

Specimen A is the beetle: 1a (legs present) → 2a (six legs) → insect.

2. A student writes a key step: “1a. It is cute … go to 2 / 1b. It is scary … go to 3”. Why would this key not work?

Key statements must be observable and measurable, not opinions. “Cute” and “scary” depend on the person looking. A good pair would be “has wings / does not have wings”.

3. Which feature separates the snake from the worm in this key?

Body covering: the snake has dry scales (reptile), the worm has a moist skin (annelid). Both have no legs, so “no legs” alone cannot separate them — a key may need several steps.
