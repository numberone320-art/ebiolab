Comparing carbon dioxide in inspired and expired air

Limewater is the classic test for CO₂: it turns milky when carbon dioxide bubbles through it. Tube A receives exhaled air (you blow). Tube B receives atmospheric air (a pump). Hold each button for the same time and compare.

Real-lab safety: do not suck limewater back through the straw — blow gently, and use a fresh mouthpiece for each person.

Expired air is about 16% oxygen — we only use part of the oxygen we breathe in.
Expired air is about 4% carbon dioxide — 100 times more than inspired air. This CO₂ is made by respiration in your cells.

1. Why does tube A turn milky faster than tube B?

Exhaled air contains about 4% CO₂ — one hundred times more than atmospheric air (0.04%). More CO₂ → the white precipitate (calcium carbonate) forms faster → limewater turns milky sooner.

2. What is the purpose of tube B in this experiment?

It is the control — it shows that ordinary air bubbling through limewater barely changes it, proving the milkiness in tube A comes from something added by your body: CO₂ from respiration.

1. When the rubber sheet is pulled DOWN, why do the balloons inflate?

Pulling down increases the volume inside the jar, so pressure inside decreases below atmospheric pressure. Air rushes in through the tube, inflating the balloons. In your body: the diaphragm contracts and flattens (and the ribs move up and out) → you breathe in.

2. What is the main limitation of this model?

The bell jar is rigid — your real rib cage moves too (up and out, using the intercostal muscles), so real breathing changes volume in two ways, not just with the diaphragm.

1. Why does breathing rate increase during exercise?

Muscles respire faster to release more energy, using more oxygen and producing more carbon dioxide. Faster, deeper breathing brings in more O₂ and removes CO₂ faster to keep blood levels steady.

2. Why count for at least 30 seconds rather than 5 seconds?

To make the measurement more reliable — a fraction of a breath missed in 5 s becomes a large error when multiplied up to a minute. Longer counts (or repeating and averaging) reduce the error.
